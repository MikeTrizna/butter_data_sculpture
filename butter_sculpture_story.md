# The Churning of the Year

### *A Dairy Epic in Twelve Slices*

*To be read aloud, slowly and gravely, beside the sculpture. Dim the lights. Lower your voice. This is a story about butter, and butter is never to be taken lightly. (It is 81% fat.)*

---

## Prologue: Three Sticks Enter

Friends. Judges. Fellow lovers of lipids. Gather 'round the table, but don't lean on it, because what rests upon it is fragile, perishable, and **udderly** important.

Before you lie the remains of **three sticks of butter**. They were ordinary once: four ounces each, 4.75 inches long, wrapped in paper and living the quiet life of the East-Coast Elgin stick. Then they were *chosen*.

Each stick was laid on its side and split **lengthwise** into **four planks**, each **0.297 inches** thick. That gave twelve planks, and twelve planks make one plank for every month. A wire passed through the cold gold three times per stick, and each cut took **0.02 inches** of butter that we will never see again. We pause for a moment of silence for the kerf.

*(Pause.)*

From each plank one shape was carved: a 19-degree wedge whose length is set by **112 years of United States Department of Agriculture data**. Every 100 million pounds of butter sitting in America's cold-storage warehouses became **1.10 inches** of sculpture. Every bar starts at a circle **0.95 inches** from the center, the hollow heart of the nation's dairy case, and reaches outward by exactly as much as the country had stockpiled.

About **a fifth** of the butter made it into the sculpture. The other four-fifths are offcuts. They are not wasted. They are *destined for dinner rolls*. Waste not, want knot.

This is the story those twelve bars tell. It is the story of a nation that hoards butter all spring, frets over it all summer, and spends it all on one Thursday in November.

**Butter believe it.**

---

## Act I: Winter. The Lean Times.

### January: *The Morning After* (135.0 million lb, 1.48 in)

The sculpture begins at the top, at twelve o'clock, where January points toward the heavens like a stick of butter praying for mercy.

The pies are eaten. The cookie tins are empty. The gravy boat has sailed. America's freezers hold an average of just **135 million pounds** of butter. That is the third-smallest bar in the circle, and it is barely more than a pat.

But do not weep for January. Out in the dairy barns, the cows are already thinking about spring. The tide is turning. The churn is about to **turn**. January isn't a low point. It's a *launch pad*.

### February: *The Slow Spread* (144.7 million lb, 1.59 in)

Up nearly **10 million pounds**. Nobody notices, because nobody is baking. February is the month butter goes to the gym, rebuilding its reserves while the nation eats salad out of guilt.

On February 1st, National Butter Day, a few brave souls salute the stick. The warehouses, unmoved, keep **piling it on**.

### March: *Stick With Me* (150.9 million lb, 1.66 in)

A modest gain of six million pounds. March is a hallway, a pause between rooms. But listen closely and you can hear it: mooing. Distant, growing mooing.

The **spring flush** is coming.

---

## Act II: Spring. The Great Flush.

### April: *The Cows Awaken* (162.3 million lb, 1.78 in)

Grass grows green. Calves arrive. Milk production climbs toward its yearly crest, and more milk means more cream, and more cream means butter **churned at a pace that would make your grandmother faint into the mashed potatoes**.

Dairy economists call this the spring flush. Poets call it *the Great Melting Upward*. In just the March-to-April stretch, CoBank found that butter stocks normally rise about **33 million pounds** on a five-year average.

The churns are working overtime, and the warehouses are filling. Things are getting **whey** out of hand.

### May: *Hoarding Season* (184.1 million lb, 2.03 in)

A gain of nearly **22 million pounds**. In the last thirty years, May has been the single most likely month for the year's peak. It was the high point in **15 of the 30 years** from 1996 to 2025.

Why hoard? Because the dairy industry knows what you don't want to admit. **The holidays are coming.** Butter churns make most of their butter in the first half of the year and stack it in the cold like squirrels with a nut-butter problem, bracing for a feast six months away.

They are not making butter for *today*. They are making butter for *Thanksgiving*. And they started in *April*.

That is not planning. That is **pie-ety**.

### June: *The Great Leap* (212.9 million lb, 2.34 in)

**+28.8 million pounds.** Over the full 112-year average, this is the steepest climb on the whole wheel. The bars on the right side of the sculpture swell, each one reaching further than the last. This bar uses **27.7%** of its plank, and the sculptor's knife was sweating.

Look at the right side of the circle and see it grow: Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr, May, Jun. Nearly eleven inches of combined ambition, rising like a soufflé, like a dinner roll, like a dairy farmer's blood pressure.

---

## Act III: Summer. The Summit.

### July: *The Crown of Cream* (224.3 million lb, 2.47 in)

**Behold.**

At the very bottom of the sculpture, at six o'clock, sits the longest bar of all. It is **2.47 inches** of pure, golden peak, the outermost point of the circle at **3.42 inches** from center. Over 112 years of data, July holds the highest average: **224.3 million pounds** of butter, frozen and waiting.

July was so long that it nearly broke the design. Inside its 1.25-inch plank, July's bar leaves only **0.02 inches** of spare room, a margin as thin as one cut of the wire. We had to carve it with the steady hands of a heart surgeon and the nerves of a turkey in mid-November.

And July carries a legend.

#### Interlude: *The Butter Mountain of 1992*

*(Lower your voice further. Lean in.)*

There was a year when the butter did not stop.

In **July 1992**, America's cold-storage warehouses held **755.8 million pounds of butter**. That's the all-time record across more than a century of data, and it is more than **three times** this sculpture's tallest bar. At our scale, that single month would be a bar **8.3 inches** long. It would not fit on the plank. It would not fit on the *table*. It would not fit in the *competition*.

Where did it come from? Not from bakers. From **the government**. Under the federal dairy price-support program, surplus milkfat was churned into butter and sold to the Commodity Credit Corporation, which stacked it in warehouses at a rate that defied gravity and good sense. By June 1992, **government-held butter alone reached 726 million pounds**. Uncle Sam was sitting on a butter mountain, and the mountain was **not melting**.

USDA cut the butter support price twice that year to stem the flood. The following year, tens of thousands of tonnes were shipped overseas to Russia. The mountain was tamed. But the record stands, untouched and **unsalted**, to this day.

Let us never forget 1992. Let us also never repeat it.

### August: *The First Crack* (215.5 million lb, 2.37 in)

And then, ever so slightly, the bars begin to **shrink**.

**−8.8 million pounds.** It's small and quiet, a hairline fracture in the golden facade. The flush has passed. The cows are hot and tired, and so is everyone else. Cream gets pulled toward ice cream. The churns slow.

The peak is behind us. The holidays are ahead. And the warehouse doors are starting to swing **open**.

---

## Act IV: Autumn. The Reckoning.

### September: *Harvest of Worry* (198.7 million lb, 2.19 in)

**−16.8 million pounds.** The leaves turn. The pumpkin spice returns. The grocery chains start sketching their holiday circulars, and somewhere in a corporate office, a buyer whispers the word *"butter"* and feels a chill that has nothing to do with the freezer.

Cream now has many suitors: eggnog, cream cheese, ice cream, and bottlers who always get first pick. Butter churns stand at the back of the line, **cap in hand**, hoping for scraps of fat.

### October: *The Drawdown Begins in Earnest* (170.9 million lb, 1.88 in)

**−27.8 million pounds.** Now the bars on the left side of the sculpture retreat fast, like the tide going out before a tsunami, a **tsunami of stuffing**.

Retailers stock up. Butter advertising climbs. The warehouse loading docks hum day and night. Every truck heading out is heavy with gold and bound for a dairy aisle near you.

The nation is **buttering itself up** for what comes next.

### November: *THANKSGIVING* (140.0 million lb, 1.54 in)

*(Rise. Remove your hat. Place your hand on your heart, or on a dinner roll.)*

This is it. This is the month. **The steepest drop on the entire wheel.**

Over 112 years of data, butter stocks fall **30.9 million pounds** from October to November on average. That's more than any other month. Over the last thirty years it's worse: **41 million pounds** gone in a single month, with November average stocks crashing to just **122 million pounds**, the lowest of the modern year.

Where does it go? Into the **turkey**, rubbed under the skin by trembling hands. Into the **mashed potatoes**, by the half-pound, without apology. Into the **pie crusts**, cold and cubed, folded with reverence. Into the **stuffing**, the **green-bean casserole**, the **sweet potatoes**, the **rolls**, and the **other rolls**. Retail butter sales in the weeks around Thanksgiving have run at **more than double** their usual volume. Some estimates say November butter sales jump nearly **30%** above an average month.

It is the Super Bowl of butter. The Olympics of fat. A national day of thanks, carried on a tide of melted gold.

In this sculpture, November's bar is short. **1.54 inches.** It is short because it *gave*. It gave everything. It emptied the warehouses so your gravy could be glossy and your pie crust could flake. November is not a small bar. **November is a sacrifice.**

Give thanks to November. It has **churned** your gratitude.

### December: *The Last Pat* (127.2 million lb, 1.40 in)

And here, at eleven o'clock, sits the smallest bar of all. **127.2 million pounds.** Only **1.40 inches**. It uses just **12.9%** of its plank. December is a sliver, the last crumb in the butter dish, scraped by a knife that knows the answer but asks anyway.

After Thanksgiving, the cookies come. The holiday breads. The latkes. The yule logs. The **butter-on-butter** baking frenzy of the darkest month. December takes what November left behind.

And yet. Look where December's bar sits: right beside January, at the top of the circle. The smallest bar points straight back to the beginning.

Because the cows never stop. The flush will come again. The churns will turn. **The wheel turns.**

---

## Epilogue: The Wheel of Butter

Step back. See it whole.

A circle **6.8 inches** across, made of twelve golden wedges that swell on the right side and shrink on the left, like a sun that rises in spring and sets at Thanksgiving. The difference between the fattest month and the leanest is **97 million pounds** on average. In a typical recent year, stocks swing about **138 million pounds** from low to high and back. It is the heartbeat of the American dairy case: inhale in spring, exhale at the holidays.

The story is still being written. In the 2020s, average stocks have run higher than in any decade since the 1990s. Production in early 2026 hit levels unseen in three decades, yet strong exports kept inventories from ballooning. As of **September 2026**, the warehouses held **295.7 million pounds**, and they are bracing once more for the November plunge.

So the next time you pass the butter at Thanksgiving, pause. Remember that it was churned in April, frozen in May, guarded through July, and released in November, all so that you could spread it on a roll and ask for seconds.

It's been a long year. Let's **butter** it.

**Thank you, and gobble bless.**

*(Bow. Do not touch the sculpture. Seriously. It's soft.)*

- All-time high: 755.8 M lb in July 1992 (747.0 M lb in June 1992).
- Over 1996 to 2025, the mean seasonal profile peaks in May (251 M lb) and bottoms in November (122 M lb). The yearly high landed in May in 15 of 30 years. The yearly low landed in December in 15 years and in November in 13.

- Decade averages: 1990s 252.5, 2000s 169.5, 2010s 215.3, 2020s (so far) 301.0 M lb.
- Latest value: 295.7 M lb, end of September 2026.

A note on the two "peaks": the sculpture uses the 112-year mean, which peaks in July. Over the last 30 years the peak has moved earlier, to May. The narration uses the sculpture's numbers for each month's bar and points out the modern pattern where it adds to the drama.
